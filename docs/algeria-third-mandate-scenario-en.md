**Disclaimer:** This document is a structured **scenario analysis**, not a prediction or legal advice. Quantitative “metrics” below are **illustrative** where not sourced—they indicate orders of magnitude for monitoring design, not verified statistics.

**Factual baseline (public record):**

- The **2020 Algerian constitutional referendum** (1 November 2020) introduced reforms widely reported as including **two-term limits** for the president, alongside other changes; **turnout was low** (~23–24% of registered voters per common reporting), which critics cite as weak popular investiture of the new rules.
- **Abdelmadjid Tebboune** was elected president in **December 2019** and **re-elected** in the **September 2024** presidential election; his **second five-year term** is understood to run from that cycle ([e.g. Reuters, AP coverage of the 2024 result](https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/algeria-court-confirms-tebboune-re-elected-president-with-8430-vote-2024-09-14/)).
- A **third consecutive mandate** would therefore **collide** with the **standard reading** of a **two-term cap** unless the constitutional text—or its **officially accepted interpretation**—is **updated** in a visible, legitimate process.

## 1.0 Executive summary and core findings

### 1.1 Analytical question

What happens to **Algeria’s domestic legitimacy**, **foreign narrative**, **security of tenure**, and **social cohesion** if the presidency moves toward a **third Tebboune mandate** without a **clear, widely accepted constitutional path** (e.g. a new referendum and revised text), or if such a path is perceived as **pro forma**?

### 1.2 Core findings (scenario logic)

1. **Constitutional vs. political time**  
   The tension is not only “two terms” on paper but **whether 2019–2024 counts as “term one” under the 2020 text** versus **political common sense** that Tebboune has already won **twice**. Any third bid without **unambiguous** constitutional renewal risks being read as **“Bouteflika logic”**—the very narrative the post-2019 order claimed to break.

2. **The “prestige paradox” (adapted)**  
   Algeria’s state brand rests on **revolutionary legitimacy**, **non-alignment**, and **stability**. A contested third mandate **raises the bar** for performance: small governance failures weigh more because the story becomes **“they changed the rules to stay.”**

3. **Opinion is multi-front**  
   Unlike a single-institution crisis, this scenario plays on **Algiers vs. regions**, **Arabic vs. Tamazight vs. French discourse**, **diaspora vs. domestic**, and **generational fracture** (young population). **Single-channel** communication fails.

4. **Secondary crises dominate**  
   The **initial event** (announcement) may be smaller than **secondary waves**: diaspora campaigns, European Parliament resolutions, youth memes, **currency/price** protests reframed as “cost of continuity,” or **factional** signaling inside the elite.

5. **Military–civilian framing**  
   Public debate rarely stays “only constitutional”; it shifts to **who guarantees the move** and **who is sidelined**—reopening **Hirak-era** memes and **deep state** narratives.

## 7.0 Risks and opportunities

### 7.1 Systemic risks

1. **Legitimacy discount** — harder to demand **sacrifice** (austerity, reform) from youth.
2. **Elite bargaining overload** — **spoils** system under **succession** uncertainty.
3. **Foreign narrative capture** — Algeria story becomes **“another Arab president for life.”**
4. **Economic coupling** — **dinar**, inflation, **parallel market** stories **merge** with politics.
5. **Low-intensity unrest** — not necessarily 2019 scale; could be **localized** + **online**.

### 7.2 Opportunities (by actor)

**For the regime (if it chooses Path A — clean referendum):**  
Chance to **re-anchor** rules, **refresh** elite, **package** with **youth jobs / housing** to **buy** passive acceptance.

**For opposition:**  
Rare **constitutional** focal point; **EU** audiences; **lawyer-led** framing.

**For external partners:**  
**Conditional engagement** narrative—**risk** of pushing Algeria toward **Eastern** partners if Western tone is **punitive-only**.

## 9.0 Conclusions and strategic recommendations

### 9.1 Regime-side (communication + governance)

1. **Prefer constitutional clarity over ambiguity** — If continuity is the goal, **ambiguous legalism** maximizes **cynicism**. A **dated, published** amendment process beats **whispered** interpretations.
2. **Bundle political time with material delivery** — **Jobs, housing, healthcare visibility**—otherwise the story is **only power**.
3. **Multi-channel, multi-language** — Arabic **dignity** narrative without insulting youth; French **legal** narrative for investors; **no** reliance on TV alone.
4. **Avoid “external enemy only”** — Overuse **backfires** when **bread** is the issue.

### 9.2 Opposition / civil society

1. **Anchor on text and process** (referendum integrity, observation, publication).
2. **Protect activists** from **hero-martyr** trap—**organization** over symbolic arrests alone.
3. **Diaspora:** target **parliamentary** and **energy** file in the EU with **specific asks** (observation, prisoners, legal reform), not only slogans.

### 9.3 External partners

1. **Separate** energy **from** values **rhetorically** but **link** in **private** diplomacy to avoid **public** humiliation cycles.
2. **Long-term:** support **independent** Algerian institutions (statistics, audit, universities) as **credible** stability.
